# Project story, interview explanations and resume bullets

*All numbers are generated from `outputs/tables/`; costs are hypothetical.*

## 60-second explanation

**PROBLEM** - Retailers need to decide how much stock to hold; I asked whether accurate, explainable demand forecasts really lead to better inventory decisions. **DATA** - Walmart's public M5 data: I modelled 18 representative item-store series (3 stores, high/medium/low demand, mostly intermittent) over 1,941 days. **METHOD** - I compared baselines, exponential smoothing, SARIMA and XGBoost on 7/14/28-day demand, then fed forecasts into a reorder-point / EOQ policy simulated day by day. **VALIDATION** - strictly chronological with rolling origins, leakage-audited (40 automated checks), choices on validation and one test evaluation. **RESULT** - best test WAPE was 37.6% / 32.8% / 29.8% at 7/14/28 days (Holt, XGBoost, XGBoost), but the leading models were statistically hard to separate. **BUSINESS IMPACT** - the forecast-driven policy lowered simulated total cost by 8.5% at a 7-day lead time versus a historical baseline (hypothetical costs, wide interval), mostly through better uncertainty estimates, and the most accurate model was usually not the cheapest (2 of 27 cells) - so I optimise the decision, not just the forecast.

## 2-minute explanation

**Problem.** Better forecasts only matter if they improve the stocking decision: too little stock loses sales, too much ties up capital. My question was whether forecasts can be converted into better inventory decisions by balancing stockout risk and holding cost.

**Data.** The M5 Walmart dataset (30,490 item-store series, daily, with prices and calendar/SNAP). The official download needs Kaggle credentials, so I used two verified public mirrors (identical SHA-256). I picked a reproducible sample using training information only: one store per state, three demand tiers, seeded draws -> 18 series; 14 are intermittent or lumpy, and zero sales cannot be told apart from stockouts, which I state as a key limitation.

**Method.** Everything is evaluated on the same target - the sum of demand over the next 7/14/28 days from daily forecast origins - with baselines (naive, seasonal naive, moving average, Croston), SES/Holt/Holt-Winters, SARIMA and a global XGBoost with leakage-safe lag, rolling, calendar, SNAP and price features. Uncertainty comes from validation residuals. The decision layer is a reorder-point + EOQ policy with safety stock z*sigma, simulated chronologically with order lead times of 3/7/14 days, comparing a historical baseline (A) with a forecast-driven policy (B).

**Validation.** Train/validation/test by date (2011-01-29-2014-10-17, then 2014-10-18-2015-08-04, then 2015-08-05-2016-05-22); all selections on validation, refit on train+validation, test evaluated once. 40 automated leakage checks, including perturbation tests and a negative control, all pass.

**Results.** Test WAPE: Holt 37.6% (7 days), XGBoost 32.8% (14), XGBoost 29.8% (28); the 28-day moving average gets 32.1% and seasonal naive 40.3%. Diebold-Mariano tests show XGBoost is never significantly better than ETS/SARIMA/MA at 14-28 days and is significantly worse than Holt, HoltWinters, SES at 7 days. SHAP shows XGBoost is mainly a smoothed recent-demand model (74% of attribution from the 7/14/28-day rolling means).

**Business impact.** In the simulation B changes total cost vs A by -2.5% / -8.5% / -13.9% at L = 3 / 7 / 14 (95%, medium penalty); the bootstrap interval at L=7 is -21% to +5%, so it is suggestive rather than conclusive. 86% of the L=7 change comes from replacing sqrt(L) sigma by validation-based sigma with the same point forecast. The most accurate model was usually not the cheapest (rank correlation -0.08 for the medium penalty), and realised cycle service (85%) fell short of the 95% target. Recommendation: simple exponential-smoothing forecasts, calibrated sigma, a cost-aware service level (LOW 90%, MEDIUM 98% (the grid edge), HIGH 98% (the grid edge) on the simulated grid) and shorter lead times.

## 5-minute technical explanation

1. **Framing.** FORECAST -> DECISION -> BUSINESS IMPACT. The target is Target_H(t) = sum_{k=1..H} y_{t+k}; the same target scores every model and equals the lead-time demand the inventory policy needs.
2. **Data and honesty about it.** M5 from verified mirrors; PROBLEM -> DIAGNOSIS -> ACTION -> REASON cleaning log; zero analysis: 30.6% of zero cells are structural (no price row), 0.32% closures (Christmas and two outages), 69.1% ambiguous -> observed sales can underestimate demand.
3. **Sampling.** TRAIN-only selection algorithm (store per state, eligibility, percentile tiers, seeded draws), 18 series, nothing tuned to results.
4. **Validation protocol.** Chronological split; daily rolling origins with complete windows inside the period; selections on validation; refit on train+validation; test once. Training rows are cut so that origin + H <= fit_end - 1.
5. **Leakage control.** Features at the origin (lag_k = y[t+1-k], windows end at t); calendar 'known-ahead' counts proven independent of sales; perturbation tests replace everything after a random cut-off and require identical features, forecasts, sigma and policy decisions up to the cut-off (40/40 pass); a deliberately leaky feature is detected (negative control).
6. **Models.** Baselines (MA window by validation: 28 days at every horizon), Croston-SBA, SES/Holt(damped)/Holt-Winters with a fallback chain, SARIMA candidates with ADF/KPSS/ACF/PACF-motivated structures chosen on validation, direct XGBoost per horizon with 24-config validation search (validation MAE gains of 1-6% over the defaults).
7. **Accuracy results.** Test WAPE table; best per horizon Holt/XGBoost/XGBoost; validation choice matched test winner at 0/3 horizons; test RMSE 1.17-2.23x validation RMSE. DM tests (HAC, HLN, Holm) show no significant XGBoost advantage; the volume-weighted (pooled WAPE) versus equal-weighted (DM panel) views can rank models differently.
8. **Explainability.** TreeSHAP on the final models: rolling means 74%/73% of attribution, SNAP 4.5%, price 3.7% (most likely a between-item effect, not an elasticity); additivity verified; three rule-selected waterfalls.
9. **Uncertainty.** sigma_L = validation RMSE of the L-day sum; Gaussian vs empirical intervals compared by split-half calibration on validation (Gaussian won); test coverage of the chosen intervals was within 5 points of nominal in 49 of 54 model/horizon/level cases, but test errors were larger than validation errors (RMSE ratio 1.17-2.23x), which later lowers realised service.
10. **Inventory model.** (s, S) with ROP = mu_L + z*sigma_L and EOQ order quantity; order placed at end of day t arrives at start of t+L+1; lost sales; daily ledger with receive -> demand -> fulfil -> stockout -> position -> order; unit-tested balance, timing, costs and causality. Policy A: trailing mean, sqrt(L) x trailing std; Policy B: model forecast + validation sigma.
11. **Results.** B vs A cost -2.5%/-8.5%/-13.9%; 21/27 cells cheaper, bootstrap intervals include zero in 27 of 27 cells; the sigma method alone accounts for 86% of the L=7 change; the gain is concentrated in the 14 intermittent series (-14.7%) while the 4 regular ones show +0.4% (L=7, 95%, MEDIUM). Cost-minimising service level: LOW 90%, MEDIUM 98% (the grid edge), HIGH 98% (the grid edge); ranges of mean cost: stockout-penalty scenario 85%, lead time 40%, service level 8%.
12. **The key insight.** Accuracy rank != cost rank (only 2/27 cells agree; mean rho LOW +0.47, MEDIUM -0.08, HIGH -0.26) because of asymmetric costs, bias, the safety-stock mechanism and validation optimism.
13. **Limitations and next steps.** Hypothetical costs, censored demand, 18 series, one test year; next: quantile/cost-aware forecasts, rolling sigma, real costs and lead-time distributions, promotions, hierarchical forecasting, pilot test.

## Resume bullets (numbers from the result tables)

* Forecasted 7/14/28-day retail demand for 18 M5 (Walmart) item-store series with nine models (naive/seasonal-naive/moving-average/Croston baselines, SES/Holt/Holt-Winters, SARIMA, XGBoost) in a chronological rolling-origin framework; best test WAPE 37.6% / 32.8% / 29.8% (Holt / XGBoost / XGBoost) vs 40.9% / 39.9% / 40.3% for seasonal naive; Diebold-Mariano tests showed the leading models statistically indistinguishable at 14-28 days.
* Built leakage-safe lag/rolling/calendar/SNAP/price features validated by 40 automated audit checks (perturbation tests plus a negative control) and used SHAP to show that 7/14/28-day rolling demand levels drive 74% of XGBoost's 7-day attributions, with SNAP-day counts (4.5%) and price (3.7%) next.
* Converted forecasts into reorder-point/EOQ policies and simulated 292 days x 18 series: the forecast-driven policy lowered pooled total cost by 8.5% (L=7, 95% target, hypothetical medium penalty; 95% bootstrap CI -21% to +5%, i.e. suggestive, not statistically significant) and lifted realised cycle service from 75% to 85% versus a historical baseline, and showed that forecast-accuracy rank did not predict cost rank (Spearman -0.08 for the medium penalty).
