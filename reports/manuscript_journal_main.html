Abstract

Background. Clinical and engineering risk prediction often rests on covariates measured repeatedly over time. Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KANs) replace network weights by learnable univariate functions and have been adapted to survival analysis with static covariates (Knottenbelt et al., 2025; Jose et al., 2025). Applied directly to trajectories, a single-layer functional KAN under the Cox loss has the model form of the additive functional Cox model (AFCM) (Cui et al., 2021), so its value has to be shown empirically: in the readability of its effects, in the tools it supports, and in honest comparisons with simpler models.

Methods. We studied functional KAN survival models whose edges act on the trajectories themselves (a separable bivariate layer, B2-S) under Cox, Weibull, log-normal and semiparametric accelerated failure time (AFT) losses, the last with an error law estimated by Kaplan–Meier (NP-KAN-AFT). Three tools were added and tested in pre-registered rounds on fresh simulation seeds: a fixed-weight symbolic distillation refit, a landmark anchor that adds the last observed values and is kept only if confirmed in both halves of the validation split, and the semiparametric error law. Comparators were AFCM, FPCA Cox, a two-stage mixed-effects Cox model, summary-based models and landmark Cox. Real-data analyses covered four clinical cohorts used in earlier rounds, two untouched joineRML cohorts and two C-MAPSS engine data sets.

Results. The confirmatory comparison with AFCM was inconclusive (B2-S Cox − AFCM in identifiable shape error +0.0065, 95% CI [−0.0506, +0.0635]), and AFCM could not be validated even with per-channel marginal-likelihood smoothing (held-out check failed, φ₁ 0.170). Both models recovered nonlinear effects far better than linear functional models. The distillation refit was finite in all 40 refits and preserved the shapes. Cross-confirmed anchoring kept the gain when the last value carried the risk and stayed within the registered margin otherwise (S-Sparse bound for Cox +0.0036). NP-KAN-AFT was better than Cox and Weibull models under log-normal errors (−0.0135 and −0.0150 in survival-curve error). Under a bimodal error law NP-KAN-AFT was better than all three parametric KAN models in every replicate (−0.0423 against log-normal), and under heavy-tailed and logistic laws better than the Cox and Weibull models (H-R22c supported). On two untouched cohorts anchoring and distillation met their margins, but one baseline fit did not converge, so the registered all-finite criterion failed (H-R22b not supported). On every clinical cohort, no functional model was clearly better than landmark Cox on the last observed values.

Conclusions. Functional KANs give readable nonlinear trajectory effects, and the three tools make them safe to summarise, to anchor and to use without choosing an error law; on small clinical cohorts with few visits a landmark model on the last values is as good, and a functional model is worth its cost mainly when the trajectory, not its last value, carries the risk.

1. Introduction

Risk models for chronic disease and for engineered systems increasingly use longitudinal covariates: repeated laboratory values, biomarkers or sensor readings. Three families of methods are established. Landmark models (van Houwelingen, 2007; van Houwelingen & Putter, 2012) use the most recent values at a prediction time; two-stage and joint models (Laird & Ware, 1982; Wulfsohn & Tsiatis, 1997; Tsiatis & Davidian, 2004; Rizopoulos, 2010) summarise each trajectory through a mixed model; functional regression models use the whole trajectory, linearly through functional principal components (Yao et al., 2005; Kong et al., 2018) or nonlinearly in the additive functional Cox model (Cui et al., 2021), which places a smooth bivariate surface F(x, t) inside the hazard. Neural survival models (Katzman et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2020) are flexible but hard to read.

Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (Liu et al., 2025) place learnable univariate functions on the edges of a network, which can be inspected, pruned and replaced by symbolic formulas. Survival versions exist for static covariates (Knottenbelt et al., 2025; Jose et al., 2025; Mastroleo et al., 2026; Boulitsakis Logothetis et al., 2026). Applied to trajectories, a single functional KAN layer under the Cox loss is a parametrisation of AFCM rather than a new model class, so any contribution must come from what the parametrisation enables and must be shown against strong comparators, including those that do not use trajectories at all.

This paper reports a sequence of pre-registered studies (Supplementary Section S2.8) organised around one thesis: functional KANs provide interpretable nonlinear survival modelling from longitudinal trajectories, with safe symbolic distillation, conditional landmark anchoring and semiparametric AFT robustness, and the same studies show where simpler landmark models suffice. Each part of the thesis was tested by a registered hypothesis with fixed criteria (Table 1), and the negative and inconclusive results are reported alongside the positive ones.

2. Methods

2.1 Data, front end and functional layers

Subject i has longitudinal channels X_id(t) observed with noise in a history window rescaled to [0, 1], scalar covariates z_i and a possibly right-censored outcome. Curves are reconstructed on a fixed grid by penalised B-spline smoothing with the smoothing parameter chosen on training subjects only. The functional layer B2-S represents the effect of channel d as a rank-two separable surface, φ_d(x, t) = Σ_m g_dm(x)h_dm(t), with every g and h a univariate B-spline KAN edge, so that the effect is read separately in the value and in time; the linear predictor is η = Σ_d ∫φ_d(X_d(t), t)dt + Σ_k ψ_k(z_k). Each model is a three-member ensemble with learning rate and weight decay tuned on the validation loss (S2.3). The ensemble’s prediction and its shape estimate are the members’ means, which is valid because η is additive in the edges; every functional effect is centred over the training data, the constant being absorbed by the baseline.

Channel selection uses a sparsity penalty during training and a channel-level score, the mean absolute deviation of the channel’s integrated effect over the training subjects relative to the largest channel score; a channel whose score falls below a threshold loses all its functional edges. Penalty and threshold are chosen by a parsimonious rule on the validation loss (S2.5). The selected model is the object that is read, summarised and, where accepted, replaced by formulas.

2.2 Survival losses

Cox. Exact partial likelihood (Cox, 1972) with Efron ties (Efron, 1977) and the Breslow baseline (Breslow, 1974). Parametric KAN-AFT. log T = μ + η + σε with Weibull or log-normal ε. NP-KAN-AFT. The location η is fitted by a smoothed Gehan rank objective and the error law is the Kaplan–Meier estimate (Kaplan & Meier, 1958) of the training residuals, the classical semiparametric AFT model with a functional KAN location; it needs no choice of error family (S5.7).

2.3 Symbolic distillation

Edges of the pruned model are fitted by a linear function, a library of affine-wrapped elementary functions and symbolic regression (Cranmer, 2023), and accepted on validation R². Substituting accepted formulas without refit reproduced the model, but an unconstrained refit drifted from it (S3.6). The distillation refit therefore refits the substituted model with a fixed penalty towards the pruned model’s predictions, rejects formulas whose argument leaves the observed domain, falls back to the spline edge on any non-finite value and rolls back if the validation loss rises (S5.2).

2.4 Conditional landmark anchoring

The anchored model adds the last observed value of each channel to B2-S, so it can use the most recent value as a landmark model does. Because anchoring helps only when the last value carries the risk, it is used conditionally: the validation split is halved, and the anchored model is kept only if it beats the unanchored model on both halves by a registered margin; otherwise the unanchored ensemble is returned unchanged (cross-confirmation, S5.7). The fallback is exact by construction, which was checked on every split.

2.5 Comparators and evaluation

Comparators were AFCM (a Python re-implementation with validation-chosen smoothing; S2.4), FPCA Cox (Kong et al., 2018), a two-stage linear mixed-effects Cox model (Laird & Ware, 1982), Cox and random survival forest (Ishwaran et al., 2008) models on trajectory summaries, and landmark Cox on the last values (van Houwelingen, 2007). In simulation the primary measures were the identifiable relative integrated squared error of the functional effect (RISE) and the survival-curve error against the oracle; on real data the inverse-probability-of-censoring weighted integrated Brier score (Graf et al., 1999) (conditional on the covariates for the clinical cohorts), Harrell’s and Uno’s concordance (Harrell et al., 1982; Uno et al., 2011) and D-calibration (Haider et al., 2020). Simulation contrasts were paired over replicates, with equivalence by two one-sided tests (Schuirmann, 1987); real-data contrasts used five outer splits by subject and the Nadeau–Bengio corrected interval (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003). Only the hypotheses of Table 1 are confirmatory.

2.6 Pre-registration and verification

Every round was registered in a decision log before its seeds or data were used, with the hypotheses, criteria, seeds, models and analysis fixed; development work used seeds or data sets set aside for it, and every run, including failed ones, is reported. Acceptance tests with fixed thresholds were written before the simulation, and a failure was never converted by changing a seed or a threshold (S2.8). Every number in this paper and in the Supplementary Material is rendered from the saved records and checked by an independent script (Appendix).

3. Simulation results

3.1 Design

The simulator generates three channels per subject on [0, 1] (a random level, trend and Fourier modes, observed with noise), one of them a null channel, and a hazard that depends on a time-homogeneous sine effect of the first channel and a time-varying effect of the second:

  • S-Add: η = ∫sin(πX₁(t)/2)dt + ∫tanh(X₂(t))(1 + t)dt + 0.3z₁ − 0.3z₂, Weibull proportional hazards (shape 1.5), so the Cox model is correctly specified. φ₁ is time-homogeneous, φ₂ time-varying. Variants add sparse visits (S-Sparse), risk carried by the last values (S-Last), and non-proportional hazards through an AFT location with non-Weibull errors (S-Misspec). Every confirmatory test used seeds not used before (S3.1, S5.1). The identifiable RISE projects out directions of the effect that no subject’s trajectory can reveal, so that a model is not penalised for a component the data cannot identify (S2.7).

3.3 Safe symbolic distillation

Distillation with a fixed penalty weight, the domain guard and the fallback was finite in all 40 refits and preserved the pruned shapes (S-Add RISE difference −0.0004; H-R16a supported), whereas its validation-tuned predecessor had failed (H-R15a). It carried over to the log-normal KAN-AFT (H-R17b, part 1). The refit is safe because it is strongly anchored to the pruned model, and for the same reason it does not improve prediction over substitution alone.

3.4 Conditional landmark anchoring

Anchoring to the last values is a trade-off. An always-anchored model was clearly better than B2-S Cox when the last values carried the risk (S-Last, −0.0176) and slightly worse when the whole trajectory did (S-Sparse, +0.0032). Gating on the validation loss did not remove that cost reliably (H-R17a), and deciding on a single confirmation split halved the data available to each model (S5.5–S5.6). Cross-confirmation on both halves of the validation split, against three-seed B2-S ensembles trained on the whole validation set, met every registered criterion for Cox, log-normal KAN-AFT and NP-KAN-AFT on fresh seeds (H-R20b; Table 6), mainly by anchoring less often: on S-Sparse the Cox model anchored in 1 of 20 replicates.

3.5 Semiparametric AFT robustness

The first step towards not assuming an error law was residual calibration: keeping the Weibull KAN-AFT’s location and scale but replacing its error law by the Kaplan–Meier estimate of the standardised training residuals. Under log-normal errors it lowered curve error below B2-S Cox (−0.0049), but not under log-logistic errors (Table 7), because the location was still fitted under the wrong likelihood. NP-KAN-AFT removes that dependence by fitting the location with a rank objective.

4. Real-data results

4.1 Cohorts

Four clinical cohorts were analysed in earlier rounds: PBC with six laboratory channels and two endpoints (Murtaugh et al., 1994; Fleming & Harrington, 1991), prothro and aids (Rizopoulos, 2010; Andersen et al., 1993; Guo & Carlin, 2004). C-MAPSS turbofan data (Saxena et al., 2008) served for calibration (FD001, and FD003, which had been analysed in an early round and so is not untouched). 5. Discussion

5.1 What the study supports

The evidence supports the thesis with clear boundaries. Functional KANs recover nonlinear trajectory effects as well as the additive functional Cox model in our simulation, though the comparison was inconclusive and the comparator unvalidated, and far better than linear functional models. Their edges can be pruned, read and summarised by formulas, and a strongly anchored distillation refit makes the summaries safe. Landmark anchoring helps when the last value carries the risk and, used conditionally with cross-confirmation, costs little when it does not. Estimating the AFT error law by Kaplan–Meier removes the need to choose an error family, and helps most when no parametric family fits the error law.

5.2 Where simpler landmark models suffice

On every clinical cohort, including the two untouched ones, no functional model was clearly better than landmark Cox on the last observed values, and on the earlier cohorts landmark Cox often had the lowest mean. These cohorts have a few hundred subjects and, in most of them, few visits before the landmark, so the most recent value carries most of what a curve could add; a functional model earns its cost when the shape or history of the trajectory carries the risk, as in our simulated scenarios, and the cross-confirmed anchor is the tool that lets one model behave like either. We read this as a statement about the information in these data, not as a failure of the method, but it was not itself tested.

5.3 Relation to prior work

The additive functional Cox model (Cui et al., 2021) already offers the model form of a single-layer functional CoxKAN, and our inconclusive comparison is consistent with that. What the KAN parametrisation adds is a uniform toolkit (pruning, symbolic summaries and composition of layers) and, in our hands, three tools that the toolkit made natural to build and test. KAN survival models for static covariates (Knottenbelt et al., 2025; Jose et al., 2025; Mastroleo et al., 2026; Boulitsakis Logothetis et al., 2026) and a KAN joint-model abstract (Lundin et al., 2025) address other input structures. Landmarking (van Houwelingen, 2007; van Houwelingen & Putter, 2012; Putter & van Houwelingen, 2022) and joint models (Wulfsohn & Tsiatis, 1997; Rizopoulos, 2010; Rizopoulos and Ghosh, 2011) remain the reference methods for dynamic prediction, and our real-data results agree with the common experience that a well-specified landmark model is hard to beat on small cohorts. The semiparametric AFT model with a Kaplan–Meier error law is classical; what we tested is its use with a functional KAN location and its robustness across error laws.

5.4 Limitations

The AFCM comparator could not be validated, so H-R12 cannot distinguish the two models. The Phase-2 tools were designed after earlier failures and tuned on few seeds, although each was then tested on fresh ones. The simulation has one data-generating design. The real cohorts are small and five splits cannot power superiority claims; FD003 is not untouched and the heart-valve cohort has about one visit per patient before the landmark. No joint model or deep dynamic model was fitted, and our literature review was targeted rather than systematic, so we make no claim of novelty. Full limitations are listed in S6.3.

5.5 Conclusions

Functional KANs are a workable, interpretable way to model nonlinear effects of longitudinal trajectories on survival. With a strongly anchored distillation refit, cross-confirmed landmark anchoring and a Kaplan–Meier error law, they can be summarised safely, adapt to whether the last value or the trajectory carries the risk, and avoid choosing an AFT error family. They were not better than simpler landmark models on small clinical cohorts, and the pre-registered record of where they help and where they do not is the contribution.
