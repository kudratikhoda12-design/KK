Abstract

Background. Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KANs) replace the weights of a neural network by
learnable univariate functions and have been adapted to survival analysis with static covariates
(Knottenbelt et al., 2025; Jose et al., 2025). Many clinical and engineering problems instead have
covariates measured repeatedly over time. A single-layer functional KAN under the Cox loss has the
same model form as the additive functional Cox model (AFCM) (Cui et al., 2021), so any contribution
must be empirical.

Methods. We report a sequence of pre-registered studies. A motivating study (framing b) embedded
KAN blocks in a functional autoencoder (KAN-FAE) and compared it with a parameter-matched MLP
autoencoder (MLP-FAE). The primary study (framing a) evaluated direct functional KAN survival
models: functional CoxKAN and functional KAN-AFT with time-homogeneous (B2-H), separable
bivariate (B2-S) and two-layer (B2-deep) functional layers; two-stage models on functional principal
component scores or trajectory summaries (B1); and stacked landmark supermodels (B3).
Comparators were a re-implementation of AFCM, an FPCA Cox model, a two-stage mixed-effects Cox
model, summary-based Cox and random survival forest models and a landmark Cox model. A
simulation study (four scenarios, 20 replicates each) had a single confirmatory test (H-R12): B2-S Cox
minus AFCM in identifiable shape error (RISE). Real-data analyses (PBC, two JM data sets, C-MAPSS)
were exploratory.

Results. H-R12 was inconclusive: B2-S Cox minus AFCM V3 was +0.0065 (95% CI [−0.0506,
+0.0635]; paired-t p 0.815; equivalence within ±0.05 not shown, TOST p 0.0634), and the AFCM
comparator failed its own single-fit acceptance check. Both direct functional models recovered
nonlinear shapes far better than a linear-in-scores FPCA model (B2-S minus B1-FPCA-1: −0.5566,
better in 20 of 20 replicates). Substituting accepted symbolic formulas preserved the learned shapes
(S-Add RISE 0.214 before and 0.212 after substitution), whereas the customary unconstrained refit
degraded them (1.604). On the real data no functional or KAN model predicted clearly better than
simple landmark baselines; every Nadeau–Bengio interval for the integrated Brier score of B2-S Cox
minus a baseline included 0. Pruning selected a stable, clinically plausible set of PBC markers, and
the leading effects were stable over 100 bootstrap fits and aligned with the pre-stated clinical
directions, with one exception (prothrombin index under B2-S). In the motivating study, KAN-FAE had
higher survival-curve error than MLP-FAE in the confirmatory test (H1: +0.0078, p 0.047), the two
were equivalent under reconstruction pre-training on fresh seeds (−0.0005, TOST p 0.0049), and the
autoencoder’s KAN edges acted on abstract features rather than on clinical trajectories.

Conclusions. Direct functional KAN survival models are a workable, interpretable parametrisation of
the additive functional Cox model, with usable pruning and symbolic summaries when the formulas
are substituted without refitting. We found no evidence of a predictive advantage over established
methods, and placing KAN blocks inside a functional autoencoder gave neither better prediction nor
readable effects. All findings beyond H1 and H-R12 are exploratory.

1. Introduction

1.1 Functional covariates in survival analysis

Repeated measurements of a biomarker or a sensor are naturally viewed as a curve X(t) observed
with noise at a few times (Ramsay & Silverman, 2005). The linear functional Cox model enters the
curve through ∫β(t)X(t)dt (Gellar et al., 2015; Kong et al., 2018), and its coefficient can also vary with
follow-up time (Du & Leroux, 2024). The additive functional Cox model (AFCM) of Cui et al. (2021)
replaces the linear term by ∫F(X(s), s)ds with a bivariate smooth F, following functional generalised
additive models (McLean et al., 2014); it is estimated with penalised tensor-product splines. Sparse,
irregular visits are commonly handled by principal components analysis through conditional
expectation (PACE) (Yao et al., 2005). Dynamic prediction uses joint models (Wulfsohn & Tsiatis, 1997;
Tsiatis & Davidian, 2004; Rizopoulos, 2010), landmarking and landmark supermodels (van
Houwelingen, 2007; van Houwelingen & Putter, 2012; Putter & van Houwelingen, 2022), or deep
models of the covariate history (Lee et al., 2020; Hu & Nan, 2025; Gao et al., 2025).

Neural Cox models such as DeepSurv (Katzman et al., 2018) gain flexibility at the cost of readable
effects. KANs (Liu et al., 2025) place a learnable univariate function on every edge, which makes each
edge a candidate for visualisation, pruning and symbolic regression. CoxKAN (Knottenbelt et al.,
2025), KAN-AFT (Jose et al., 2025), SurvKAN (Mastroleo et al., 2026) and KAPLAN (Boulitsakis
Logothetis et al., 2026) all use static covariates; their time-varying elements concern effects that
change with follow-up time, not covariates measured over time. A KAN joint model with yearly
landmarks was found only as a conference abstract (Lundin et al., 2025), and F-KAN feeds functional
principal component scores to a KAN for a scalar response (Qin & Li, 2026).

1.2 Architectural motivation: KAN blocks inside a functional autoencoder

The project began (Rounds 1–9) with a different architecture: a functional autoencoder (Wu et al.,
2024) whose encoder used KAN blocks, with an AFT survival head on the latent representation (KAN-
FAE), against a parameter-matched MLP encoder (MLP-FAE). The hope was that a KAN encoder would
give both better prediction and readable effects. Two findings ended that line (Section 3.7 gives the
numbers).

   1. No predictive advantage. The conventional MLP-FAE first had to pass a pre-registered
      learnability gate; every design failed it until Round 8 (Table 1 lists the gate looks with saved
      verdict records). Under the configuration that passed, the single confirmatory test (H1) found
      KAN-FAE worse than MLP-FAE in survival-curve error. With reconstruction pre-training (strategy
      S3) the two were indistinguishable, and a fresh-seed re-test in Round 12 declared them
      equivalent within a pre-registered margin.
   2. No clinical readability. In an autoencoder the KAN edges act on abstract front-end features
      and feed latent units that a further head combines into risk. Pruning removed almost no edge,
      and the symbolic formulas, though accurate, described maps between abstract coordinates
      rather than effects of a clinical variable.

2. Models and estimation

2.1 Data and notation

Subject i has D longitudinal channels X_id(t) observed with noise at times t_ij in a history window,
rescaled to [0, 1], scalar covariates z_i, and a possibly right-censored outcome (Y_i, δ_i). Curves are
reconstructed on a fixed grid of 50 points, either by per-subject penalised B-spline smoothing (12
basis functions; smoothing parameter chosen on held-out points of training subjects) or by PACE (Yao
et al., 2005). Integrals are trapezoid quadratures on the grid. Inputs to every KAN are standardised
with training statistics so that each edge can be read in original units after back-transformation.

2.2 Survival losses

Cox. The linear predictor η_i has no intercept. The negative log partial likelihood (Cox, 1972) is
computed exactly under the Breslow and Efron tie corrections (Breslow, 1974; Efron, 1977) (Efron by
default), and the baseline cumulative hazard is the Breslow estimator, giving S(t | x) =
exp(−Λ̂₀(t)e^η). CoxKAN’s published fast approximation of the loss is not used; this is a disclosed
deviation. Acceptance tests confirmed the loss against lifelines and a direct formula (difference
below 1e-8) and the baseline against lifelines (below 1e-6).

Accelerated failure time (KAN-AFT). log T = μ + η + σε with a Weibull (Gumbel ε) likelihood.
Residual calibration (b2s_aft_rc) keeps the fitted location and scale but predicts S(t | x) = Ŝ_ε((log t
− μ̂ − η̂)/σ̂), where Ŝ_ε is the Kaplan–Meier estimate (Kaplan & Meier, 1958) of the standardised
training residuals, so that the error law is not assumed.

2.3 Model families

      B1 (two-stage interpretable coordinates). B1-FPCA: functional principal component scores
      per channel (95% variance explained, at most 5 components; PACE as an alternative front end
      in the sparse simulation), with z, feed a one-layer additive KAN (B1-FPCA-1) or a two-layer KAN
      [p → 4 → 1] (B1-FPCA-2). B1-SUM: per-channel summaries (last value, mean, OLS slope, residual
      SD) feed the same heads.
      B2 (functional KAN layer on X(t)).
             B2-H (time-homogeneous): η = Σ_d ∫φ_d(X_d(t))dt + Σ_k ψ_k(z_k).
             B2-S (separable bivariate, rank 2): φ_d(x, t) = Σ_m g_dm(x)h_dm(t), with every g and h a
             univariate KAN edge; the surface is read in x and t separately.
             B2-deep: the functional summaries ∫g_dm(X_d(t))h_dm(t)dt feed a second KAN layer
             [D·M + p → 4 → 1], which can represent interactions between channels.
             Each φ_d is centred over the training data (the constant is absorbed by the baseline).
             The primary input scale is standardised X; a quantile-transformed input (Cui et al., 2021)
             is a sensitivity analysis.
      B3 (stacked landmark supermodel). For each landmark t_L and subject at risk, features
      from the history window before t_L only, outcome administratively censored at t_L + w, one
      row per (subject, landmark); a CoxKAN with a landmark-stratified baseline and t_L as an
      additional input edge (van Houwelingen & Putter, 2012). The linear comparator is the same
      supermodel with linear effects (b3_cox).

KAN layers are B-spline KANs (grid 5, order 3, SiLU base). Every KAN model is a three-member
ensemble: learning rate {1e-3, 3e-3} × weight decay {1e-4, 1e-3} tuned on the validation loss with
seed 0, then seeds 0–2 trained at the chosen setting (Adam, early stopping with patience 100, at
most 3,000 epochs). The ensemble’s prediction and shape estimate are the members’ means, which
is valid because η is additive in φ; the seed-0 member is also reported.
2.4 Comparators

      AFCM (Cui et al., 2021): ∫F(X(s), s)ds with F a 10 × 10 tensor-product cubic B-spline, difference
      penalties in x and t, penalised partial likelihood. R was not available, so AFCM is a Python re-
      implementation with smoothing parameters chosen by validation partial likelihood instead of
      REML in mgcv. V3 (the primary comparator) uses a widened λ_x grid {1e-7, …, 1e1} × λ_t {1e-
      4, 1e-2, 1, 1e2, 1e4}, penalties normalised by basis size and a first- plus second-order
      difference penalty in t. V0, the earlier version, is a sensitivity row. Validation caveat: V3 was
      chosen on one development seed and then failed its pre-registered single-fit acceptance check
      (CK-6c): on a fresh seed its identifiable RISE for the time-homogeneous effect was 0.247
      against a threshold of 0.15. AFCM is therefore an unvalidated comparator throughout.
      fpca_cox: linear Cox on FPCA scores (the FLCRM model class (Kong et al., 2018)).
      lme2_cox: per-channel linear mixed model with random intercept and slope (Laird & Ware,
      1982), fitted by EM; the BLUPs at the landmark enter a Cox model (a two-stage, joint-model-
      style baseline; JM itself was not available).
      sum_cox, sum_rsf: Cox and random survival forest (Ishwaran et al., 2008) on trajectory
      summaries.
      landmark_cox: Cox on the last observed values at the landmark plus z (van Houwelingen,
      2007).
      static CoxKAN: CoxKAN on the last observed values plus z. flat_ae: an autoencoder on the
      curve values on the grid, flattened into one vector (sparse simulation only).

2.5 Pruning and channel selection

Training adds λ·(|Φ|₁ + 2·entropy(Φ) + |C|₁) to the loss. For channel selection the score of channel d is
the mean absolute deviation of its integrated functional effect over the training subjects, normalised
by the largest channel score; a channel scoring below θ_rel loses all its functional edges, and a
pruned edge’s mean is absorbed by the baseline. The grid is λ {0, 1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 3e-2} × θ_rel {0,
0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25}, each point fine-tuned for 200 epochs with the mask fixed.
The selection rule is parsimonious: among grid points within 0.01 (mean negative log partial
likelihood per event) of the minimum validation loss, the largest θ_rel, then the largest λ
(select_lambda_theta). A channel is “selected” if any of its edges survives.

2.6 Symbolic extraction, substitution and refit

Each surviving edge of the pruned model is fitted in three stages: a linear function, a library of affine-
wrapped elementary functions, and PySR symbolic regression (Cranmer, 2023) (60 s per edge; only
edges carrying at least 5% of their layer’s score, at most 10 per model). A formula is accepted if its
validation R² exceeds 0.99. Accepted formulas replace their edges with trainable affine parameters.
The registered procedure then refits all parameters, which we did with unconstrained L-BFGS.

Methodological finding. In the simulation (Section 3.6) the substituted model without refit
reproduced the pruned model’s shapes, whereas the unconstrained refit drifted far from them, and in
a few fits a formula left its domain (for example the logarithm of a negative argument) and produced
non-finite risks. The owner therefore fixed, before the real-data study, that the primary symbolic
model is the substituted model without refit; the refitted model is reported as secondary (D128). The
real data confirmed the pattern (Section 4.3).

2.7 Metrics and statistical analysis

      Shape. Relative integrated squared error (RISE) of the centred effect against the centred truth,
      weighted by the empirical density of (X(t), t) in the test data. Because some directions of φ
      cannot affect any subject’s η (for example a component linear in x when ∫X dt is nearly
      constant across subjects), the primary metric is the identifiable RISE: error and truth are
      projected orthogonally to the directions of a 10 × 10 tensor B-spline space whose functional
        has relative variance below 3e-8 on the test curves. The time-homogeneous estimate of B2-H
        against the homogeneous truth uses a one-dimensional RISE. The primary shape summary is
        the mean over the two active channels.
        Survival. Survival-curve error against the simulation oracle; integrated Brier score (IBS) with
        inverse probability of censoring weights (Graf et al., 1999) (marginal, and conditional on z on
        real data); Harrell’s C (Harrell et al., 1982); Uno’s C (Uno et al., 2011) (τ the 90% quantile); D-
        calibration p (Haider et al., 2020). For B3: Brier score at the horizon, C within the horizon, and
        time-dependent AUC only when at least 10 cases and 10 controls exist.
        Selection. Recall of the active channels and false discovery rate (the null channel selected).
        Inference. Simulation: paired over replicates (mean difference, 95% t-interval, paired t, exact
        Wilcoxon, sign counts); equivalence by two one-sided tests (TOST) (Schuirmann, 1987). Real
        data: 5 outer splits by subject with the Nadeau–Bengio corrected resampled t-interval (Nadeau
        & Bengio, 2003). Only H1 and H-R12 are confirmatory; everything else is exploratory without
        multiplicity correction.
        Interpretability (real data). Stability: mean pairwise R² (squared cosine, so sign- and scale-
        invariant) and mean pairwise cosine of the centred time-averaged effects over 5 splits × 20
        bootstrap resamples, on a common 50-point grid between the 5th and 95th percentiles. Clinical
        alignment: an effect is aligned if it is monotone in the pre-stated direction in at least 80% of
        these fits.

3. Simulation study

3.1 Design

The simulator (DGP v2) generates n = 1,000 subjects per replicate with three channels on t ∈ [0, 1]:
a random level, a random linear trend and four Fourier modes per channel, observed with Gaussian
noise at 40 dense times, or at 3–8 random visits in the sparse scenario. Channel 3 is a null channel.
Train/validation/test splits are 60/20/20, and censoring is about 30%. Each scenario has 20 replicates
on fresh seeds (S-Add 3000–3019, S-Int 3100–3119, S-Sparse 3200–3219, S-Misspec 3300–3319, S-
Int/P0 3400–3419).

        S-Add: η = ∫sin(πX₁(t)/2)dt + ∫tanh(X₂(t))(1 + t)dt + 0.3z₁ − 0.3z₂, Weibull proportional
        hazards (shape 1.5), so the Cox model is correctly specified. φ₁ is time-homogeneous, φ₂ time-
        varying.
        S-Int: S-Add’s η plus 0.8 times the product of the two channels’ functional summaries (a cross-
        channel interaction that no additive model can represent).
      S-Sparse: S-Add with 3–8 visits per subject; B-spline smoothing against PACE.
      S-Misspec: S-Add’s η as an AFT location, log T = 1 − η/1.5 + 0.5ε with log-normal or log-
      logistic ε (non-PH; the Cox model is misspecified).
      S-Int/P0: the Round-1 simulator setting P0, re-testing KAN-FAE against MLP-FAE under strategy
      S3.

The study produced 1243 model records; 3 pruning/symbolic cells failed on a non-finite refit, were
rerun after an evaluation-only harness fix, and are reported with their original failed records.

4. Real-data applications

4.1 Data and cohorts

       PBC (pbcseq, R package survival (Therneau et al., 2026; Therneau & Grambsch, 2000;
       Murtaugh et al., 1994; Fleming & Harrington, 1991)): six laboratory channels (log bilirubin,
       albumin, log alkaline phosphatase, log AST, platelets, log prothrombin time), age and sex;
       single landmark at 730 days with the whole history; endpoint E2 death or transplantation
       (primary) and E1 death (sensitivity).
       prothro (R package JM (Rizopoulos, 2010; Andersen et al., 1993)): prothrombin index;
       prednisone versus placebo; landmark 1 year.
       aids (JM (Rizopoulos, 2010; Guo & Carlin, 2004)): CD4; drug, sex, previous opportunistic
       infection, AZT failure; landmark 6 months.
       C-MAPSS FD001 (Saxena & Goebel, 2008; Saxena et al., 2008): turbofan run-to-failure sensor
       windows (Option B), a secondary data set used for the calibration question only.

Data-hygiene disclosures. The aids CD4 values are already on the square-root scale (non-integer
values, maximum 24.1), although the package documentation calls them counts, so they were used
as provided. aids has 1,405 rows, not the 1,408 its documentation states. prothro has 488 patients; its
documentation gives both 488 and 467.

5. Discussion

5.1 Principal findings

   1. Direct functional KANs are a workable parametrisation of the additive functional Cox
      model, not a better one. B2-S and AFCM V3 recovered the S-Add shapes to a similar degree
      and could not be separated by the confirmatory test; both were far better than linear-in-scores
      two-stage models when the effect is nonlinear.
   2. Interpretability tools work when used conservatively. Channel-level pruning found the
      active channels (with imperfect removal of the null one); symbolic formulas substituted without
      refit reproduced the model; leading real-data effects were stable and pointed in clinically
      expected directions. An unconstrained refit after substitution should be avoided or constrained.
   3. No predictive advantage on small real cohorts. Simple landmark models were at least as
      good; with so few visits per subject, the last observed value plausibly carried most of the
      information (an interpretation, not a tested finding).
   4. KAN blocks inside an autoencoder did not help. KAN-FAE was worse than or equivalent to
      MLP-FAE in every comparison, and its edges were not readable as clinical effects.

5.2 Relation to prior work

Our results are consistent with the view that the additive functional Cox model (Cui et al., 2021)
already captures the model form a single-layer functional CoxKAN offers. What KAN parametrisation
adds is a uniform toolkit of pruning and symbolic summaries and the option to compose layers; in our
interaction scenario the composed B2-deep did not use that option to advantage. KAN survival
models with static covariates (Knottenbelt et al., 2025; Jose et al., 2025; Mastroleo et al., 2026;
Boulitsakis Logothetis et al., 2026) and the KAN joint model abstract (Lundin et al., 2025) address
different input structures, and we make no claim relative to work we could read only in abstract.
5.3 Limitations

      Unvalidated comparator. AFCM V3 failed its single-fit acceptance check; its shape recovery
      depends on how the validation-chosen penalties fall on a given data set (one λ_t shared by a
      time-homogeneous and a time-varying channel trades one against the other). AFCM is a Python
      re-implementation without REML.
      One simulator, 20 replicates. H-R12 rests on one DGP with three channels and n = 1,000.
      Small real-data cohorts with few visits, so all intervals are wide; five outer splits give
      limited resolution.
      PACE failed on one PBC split (non-finite curves on a gappy visit design); the PACE sensitivity
      row has 4 PBC E2 splits.
      Coarse clinical alignment. Monotonicity over the 5th–95th percentiles is a crude check;
      clinical sign-off is external, as is the choice of the PBC endpoint.
      No joint model or deep dynamic model (JM, Dynamic-DeepHit) was fitted; the two-stage
      mixed-effects Cox model stands in for the joint-model class.
      Framing (b) is conditional on configurations selected and gated for the MLP; it shows that
      the KAN encoder did not help under these configurations, not that it cannot.
      Targeted literature review. No systematic review was done, so no novelty claim is made.

5.4 Reproducibility

All code, decision logs, pre-registrations, raw records and this manuscript are in one repository. Public
data were obtained from CRAN (survival 3.8-12, JM 1.5-2) and NASA’s prognostics repository, with
checksums in the run manifest. Each round’s plan was committed before its seeds were used. Every
number in this manuscript is rendered from the saved records, logged with its source, and re-checked
by an independent script and a unit test (Appendix A).


6. Conclusions

Functional CoxKAN and KAN-AFT models that apply KAN edges directly to longitudinal trajectories
recover nonlinear functional effects, support channel selection and give accurate symbolic summaries
when the formulas are substituted without refitting. In our pre-registered comparisons they were not
better than the additive functional Cox model (whose re-implementation could not itself be validated)
or than simple landmark baselines on small clinical cohorts, and burying KAN blocks inside a
functional autoencoder gave neither better prediction nor clinically readable effects. These negative
and equivalence findings, with the full record behind them, are the contribution.
