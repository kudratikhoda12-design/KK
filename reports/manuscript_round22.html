Abstract

Background. Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KANs) replace the weights of a neural network by learnable univariate functions and have been adapted to survival analysis with static covariates (Knottenbelt et al., 2025; Jose et al., 2025). Many clinical and engineering problems instead have covariates measured repeatedly over time. A single-layer functional KAN under the Cox loss has the same model form as the additive functional Cox model (AFCM) (Cui et al., 2021), so any contribution must be empirical.

Methods. We report a sequence of pre-registered studies. A motivating study (framing b) embedded KAN blocks in a functional autoencoder (KAN-FAE) and compared it with a parameter-matched MLP autoencoder (MLP-FAE). The primary study (framing a) evaluated direct functional KAN survival models: functional CoxKAN and functional KAN-AFT with time-homogeneous (B2-H), separable bivariate (B2-S) and two-layer (B2-deep) functional layers; two-stage models on functional principal component scores or trajectory summaries (B1); and stacked landmark supermodels (B3). Comparators were a re-implementation of AFCM, an FPCA Cox model, a two-stage mixed-effects Cox model, summary-based Cox and random survival forest models and a landmark Cox model. A simulation study (four scenarios, 20 replicates each) had a single confirmatory test (H-R12): B2-S Cox minus AFCM in identifiable shape error (RISE). Real-data analyses (PBC, two JM data sets, C-MAPSS) were exploratory. A second phase (Rounds 15–22) tested, on fresh simulation seeds and with pre-registered multi-part criteria, a distillation refit for the symbolic summaries, a landmark-anchored functional model that adds the last observed values to B2-S, a two-way gate that can fall back to B2-S, and both tools in a log-normal KAN-AFT; the last round added a semiparametric functional KAN-AFT whose error law is estimated by Kaplan–Meier, and a confirmation of anchoring in both halves of the validation split; the final round applied the locked versions of these methods to the real data sets.

Results. H-R12 was inconclusive: B2-S Cox minus AFCM V3 was +0.0065 (95% CI [−0.0506, +0.0635]; paired-t p 0.815; equivalence within ±0.05 not shown, TOST p 0.0634), and the AFCM comparator failed its own single-fit acceptance check. Both direct functional models recovered nonlinear shapes far better than a linear-in-scores FPCA model (B2-S minus B1-FPCA-1: −0.5566, better in 20 of 20 replicates). Substituting accepted symbolic formulas preserved the learned shapes (S-Add RISE 0.214 before and 0.212 after substitution), whereas the customary unconstrained refit degraded them (1.604). On the real data no functional or KAN model predicted clearly better than simple landmark baselines; every Nadeau–Bengio interval for the integrated Brier score of B2-S Cox minus a baseline included 0. In Phase 2, a distillation refit with a fixed anchor weight, a NaN-safe fallback and a bound on formula arguments kept all 40 refits finite and preserved the pruned shapes (H-R16a supported, S-Add RISE difference −0.0004; its validation-tuned predecessor H-R15a had failed). A landmark-anchored model that adds the last observed values to B2-S was clearly better than B2-S Cox when risk depended on the last values (S-Last, −0.0176) but slightly worse when it depended on the whole trajectory (S-Sparse, +0.0032), so H-R15b and H-R16b were not supported. A two-way gate that falls back to B2-S unless anchoring wins on validation kept the S-Last gain but again missed the S-Sparse margin on fresh seeds (upper bound +0.0053; H-R17a not supported), while for a log-normal KAN-AFT the distillation refit and a more conservative two-way gate met every registered criterion (H-R17b supported). Deciding on held-out validation subjects whether to anchor (Round 18) met every registered criterion for Cox and for the AFT model (H-R18a and H-R18b supported; S-Sparse bound for Cox +0.0028), although against B2-S trained on the whole validation set the S-Sparse bound was wider; refitting the anchored model on the whole validation set did not close that gap in a development check, so no formal test was run (Round 19). In Round 20 the semiparametric KAN-AFT was better than B2-S Cox and a Weibull KAN-AFT under log-normal errors (H-R20a supported; −0.0135 and −0.0150) but not shown to be better than the correctly specified log-normal KAN-AFT (−0.0010), and confirming anchoring in both halves of the validation split against three-seed B2-S ensembles met every registered criterion against B2-S trained on the whole validation set, for Cox and both AFT models (H-R20b supported; S-Sparse bound for Cox +0.0036). On the real data (Round 21) the semiparametric KAN-AFT was finite, D-calibrated and close to the other B2-S models, but on the primary PBC endpoint it did not match the Weibull KAN-AFT (+0.0044 in mean conditional IBS; H-R21a not supported), and cross-confirmed anchoring never cost more than the registered margin on any clinical data set (H-R21b supported). On two cohorts not used before (Round 22) the locked models were again close to the baselines, with every interval including 0, and anchoring and distillation met their margins, but one baseline fit failed, so the registered all-finite criterion was not met (H-R22b not supported); in simulation the semiparametric KAN-AFT was better than all three parametric KAN models under a bimodal error law, and than the Cox and Weibull models under heavy-tailed and logistic laws (H-R22c supported); a per-channel marginal-likelihood AFCM failed its held-out check, so H-R12 stays inconclusive. Pruning selected a stable, clinically plausible set of PBC markers, and the leading effects were stable over 100 bootstrap fits and aligned with the pre-stated clinical directions, with one exception (prothrombin index under B2-S). In the motivating study, KAN-FAE had higher survival-curve error than MLP-FAE in the confirmatory test (H1: +0.0078, p 0.047), the two were equivalent under reconstruction pre-training on fresh seeds (−0.0005, TOST p 0.0049), and the autoencoder’s KAN edges acted on abstract features rather than on clinical trajectories.

Conclusions. Direct functional KAN survival models are a workable, interpretable parametrisation of the additive functional Cox model, with usable pruning and symbolic summaries when the formulas are substituted without refitting. We found no evidence of a predictive advantage over established methods, and placing KAN blocks inside a functional autoencoder gave neither better prediction nor readable effects. A strongly anchored refit makes the symbolic summaries safe to refit but does not improve on substitution alone, for Cox and AFT models alike; anchoring to the last observed values helps when they carry the risk and is hard to switch off reliably when they do not, unless the decision is confirmed on held-out data in both directions. Estimating the AFT error law by Kaplan–Meier removes the need to choose an error family. All findings beyond the registered hypotheses (H1, H-R12, H-R15a/b, H-R16a/b, H-R17a/b, H-R18a/b, H-R20a/b, H-R21a/b/c, H-R22b/c) are exploratory.

1. Introduction

1.1 Functional covariates in survival analysis

Repeated measurements of a biomarker or a sensor are naturally viewed as a curve X(t) observed with noise at a few times (Ramsay & Silverman, 2005). The linear functional Cox model enters the curve through ∫β(t)X(t)dt (Gellar et al., 2015; Kong et al., 2018), and its coefficient can also vary with follow-up time (Du & Leroux, 2024). The additive functional Cox model (AFCM) of Cui et al. (2021) replaces the linear term by ∫F(X(s), s)ds with a bivariate smooth F, following functional generalised additive models (McLean et al., 2014); it is estimated with penalised tensor-product splines. Sparse, irregular visits are commonly handled by principal components analysis through conditional expectation (PACE) (Yao et al., 2005). Dynamic prediction uses joint models (Wulfsohn & Tsiatis, 1997; Tsiatis & Davidian, 2004; Rizopoulos, 2010), landmarking and landmark supermodels (van Houwelingen, 2007; van Houwelingen & Putter, 2012; Putter & van Houwelingen, 2022), or deep models of the covariate history (Lee et al., 2020; Hu & Nan, 2025; Gao et al., 2025).

Neural Cox models such as DeepSurv (Katzman et al., 2018) gain flexibility at the cost of readable effects. KANs (Liu et al., 2025) place a learnable univariate function on every edge, which makes each edge a candidate for visualisation, pruning and symbolic regression. CoxKAN (Knottenbelt et al., 2025), KAN-AFT (Jose et al., 2025), SurvKAN (Mastroleo et al., 2026) and KAPLAN (Boulitsakis Logothetis et al., 2026) all use static covariates; their time-varying elements concern effects that change with follow-up time, not covariates measured over time. A KAN joint model with yearly landmarks was found only as a conference abstract (Lundin et al., 2025), and F-KAN feeds functional principal component scores to a KAN for a scalar response (Qin & Li, 2026).

1.2 Architectural motivation: KAN blocks inside a functional autoencoder

The project began (Rounds 1–9) with a different architecture: a functional autoencoder (Wu et al., 2024) whose encoder used KAN blocks, with an AFT survival head on the latent representation (KAN-FAE), against a parameter-matched MLP encoder (MLP-FAE). The hope was that a KAN encoder would give both better prediction and readable effects. Two findings ended that line (Section 3.7 gives the numbers).

  1. No predictive advantage. The conventional MLP-FAE first had to pass a pre-registered learnability gate; every design failed it until Round 8 (Table 1 lists the gate looks with saved verdict records). Under the configuration that passed, the single confirmatory test (H1) found KAN-FAE worse than MLP-FAE in survival-curve error. With reconstruction pre-training (strategy S3) the two were indistinguishable, and a fresh-seed re-test in Round 12 declared them equivalent within a pre-registered margin.
  2. No clinical readability. In an autoencoder the KAN edges act on abstract front-end features and feed latent units that a further head combines into risk. Pruning removed almost no edge, and the symbolic formulas, though accurate, described maps between abstract coordinates rather than effects of a clinical variable.

2. Models and estimation

2.1 Data and notation

Subject i has D longitudinal channels X_id(t) observed with noise at times t_ij in a history window, rescaled to [0, 1], scalar covariates z_i, and a possibly right-censored outcome (Y_i, δ_i). Curves are reconstructed on a fixed grid of 50 points, either by per-subject penalised B-spline smoothing (12 basis functions; smoothing parameter chosen on held-out points of training subjects) or by PACE (Yao et al., 2005). Integrals are trapezoid quadratures on the grid. Inputs to every KAN are standardised with training statistics so that each edge can be read in original units after back-transformation.

2.2 Survival losses

Cox. The linear predictor η_i has no intercept. The negative log partial likelihood (Cox, 1972) is computed exactly under the Breslow and Efron tie corrections (Breslow, 1974; Efron, 1977) (Efron by default), and the baseline cumulative hazard is the Breslow estimator, giving S(t | x) = exp(−Λ̂₀(t)e^η). CoxKAN’s published fast approximation of the loss is not used; this is a disclosed deviation. Acceptance tests confirmed the loss against lifelines and a direct formula (difference below 1e-8) and the baseline against lifelines (below 1e-6).

Accelerated failure time (KAN-AFT). log T = μ + η + σε with a Weibull (Gumbel ε) likelihood. Residual calibration (b2s_aft_rc) keeps the fitted location and scale but predicts S(t | x) = Ŝ_ε((log t − μ̂ − η̂)/σ̂), where Ŝ_ε is the Kaplan–Meier estimate (Kaplan & Meier, 1958) of the standardised training residuals, so that the error law is not assumed.

2.3 Model families

  • B1 (two-stage interpretable coordinates). B1-FPCA: functional principal component scores per channel (95% variance explained, at most 5 components; PACE as an alternative front end in the sparse simulation), with z, feed a one-layer additive KAN (B1-FPCA-1) or a two-layer KAN [p → 4 → 1] (B1-FPCA-2). B1-SUM: per-channel summaries (last value, mean, OLS slope, residual SD) feed the same heads.
  • B2 (functional KAN layer on X(t)).
    • B2-H (time-homogeneous): η = Σ_d ∫φ_d(X_d(t))dt + Σ_k ψ_k(z_k).
    • B2-S (separable bivariate, rank 2): φ_d(x, t) = Σ_m g_dm(x)h_dm(t), with every g and h a univariate KAN edge; the surface is read in x and t separately.
    • B2-deep: the functional summaries ∫g_dm(X_d(t))h_dm(t)dt feed a second KAN layer [D·M + p → 4 → 1], which can represent interactions between channels.
    • Each φ_d is centred over the training data (the constant is absorbed by the baseline). The primary input scale is standardised X; a quantile-transformed input (Cui et al., 2021) is a sensitivity analysis.
  • B3 (stacked landmark supermodel). For each landmark t_L and subject at risk, features from the history window before t_L only, outcome administratively censored at t_L + w, one row per (subject, landmark); a CoxKAN with a landmark-stratified baseline and t_L as an additional input edge (van Houwelingen & Putter, 2012). The linear comparator is the same supermodel with linear effects (b3_cox).

KAN layers are B-spline KANs (grid 5, order 3, SiLU base). Every KAN model is a three-member ensemble: learning rate {1e-3, 3e-3} × weight decay {1e-4, 1e-3} tuned on the validation loss with seed 0, then seeds 0–2 trained at the chosen setting (Adam, early stopping with patience 100, at most 3,000 epochs). The ensemble’s prediction and shape estimate are the members’ means, which is valid because η is additive in φ; the seed-0 member is also reported.

2.4 Comparators

  • AFCM (Cui et al., 2021): ∫F(X(s), s)ds with F a 10 × 10 tensor-product cubic B-spline, difference penalties in x and t, penalised partial likelihood. R was not available, so AFCM is a Python re-implementation with smoothing parameters chosen by validation partial likelihood instead of REML in mgcv. V3 (the primary comparator) uses a widened λ_x grid {1e-7, …, 1e1} × λ_t {1e-4, 1e-2, 1, 1e2, 1e4}, penalties normalised by basis size and a first- plus second-order difference penalty in t. V0, the earlier version, is a sensitivity row. Validation caveat: V3 was chosen on one development seed and then failed its pre-registered single-fit acceptance check (CK-6c): on a fresh seed its identifiable RISE for the time-homogeneous effect was 0.247 against a threshold of 0.15. AFCM is therefore an unvalidated comparator throughout.
  • fpca_cox: linear Cox on FPCA scores (the FLCRM model class (Kong et al., 2018)).
  • lme2_cox: per-channel linear mixed model with random intercept and slope (Laird & Ware, 1982), fitted by EM; the BLUPs at the landmark enter a Cox model (a two-stage, joint-model-style baseline; JM itself was not available).
  • sum_cox, sum_rsf: Cox and random survival forest (Ishwaran et al., 2008) on trajectory summaries.
  • landmark_cox: Cox on the last observed values at the landmark plus z (van Houwelingen, 2007).
  • static CoxKAN: CoxKAN on the last observed values plus z. flat_ae: an autoencoder on the curve values on the grid, flattened into one vector (sparse simulation only).

2.5 Pruning and channel selection

Training adds λ·(|Φ|₁ + 2·entropy(Φ) + |C|₁) to the loss. For channel selection the score of channel d is the mean absolute deviation of its integrated functional effect over the training subjects, normalised by the largest channel score; a channel scoring below θ_rel loses all its functional edges, and a pruned edge’s mean is absorbed by the baseline. The grid is λ {0, 1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 3e-2} × θ_rel {0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25}, each point fine-tuned for 200 epochs with the mask fixed. The selection rule is parsimonious: among grid points within 0.01 (mean negative log partial likelihood per event) of the minimum validation loss, the largest θ_rel, then the largest λ (select_lambda_theta). A channel is “selected” if any of its edges survives.

2.6 Symbolic extraction, substitution and refit

Each surviving edge of the pruned model is fitted in three stages: a linear function, a library of affine-wrapped elementary functions, and PySR symbolic regression (Cranmer, 2023) (60 s per edge; only edges carrying at least 5% of their layer’s score, at most 10 per model). A formula is accepted if its validation R² exceeds 0.99. Accepted formulas replace their edges with trainable affine parameters. The registered procedure then refits all parameters, which we did with unconstrained L-BFGS.

Methodological finding. In the simulation (Section 3.6) the substituted model without refit reproduced the pruned model’s shapes, whereas the unconstrained refit drifted far from them, and in a few fits a formula left its domain (for example the logarithm of a negative argument) and produced non-finite risks. The owner therefore fixed, before the real-data study, that the primary symbolic model is the substituted model without refit; the refitted model is reported as secondary (D128). The real data confirmed the pattern (Section 4.3). Phase 2 tested a constrained refit (Section 5.2).

2.7 Metrics and statistical analysis

  • Shape. Relative integrated squared error (RISE) of the centred effect against the centred truth, weighted by the empirical density of (X(t), t) in the test data. Because some directions of φ cannot affect any subject’s η (for example a component linear in x when ∫X dt is nearly constant across subjects), the primary metric is the identifiable RISE: error and truth are projected orthogonally to the directions of a 10 × 10 tensor B-spline space whose functional has relative variance below 3e-8 on the test curves. The time-homogeneous estimate of B2-H against the homogeneous truth uses a one-dimensional RISE. The primary shape summary is the mean over the two active channels.
  • Survival. Survival-curve error against the simulation oracle; integrated Brier score (IBS) with inverse probability of censoring weights (Graf et al., 1999) (marginal, and conditional on z on real data); Harrell’s C (Harrell et al., 1982); Uno’s C (Uno et al., 2011) (τ the 90% quantile); D-calibration p (Haider et al., 2020). For B3: Brier score at the horizon, C within the horizon, and time-dependent AUC only when at least 10 cases and 10 controls exist.
  • Selection. Recall of the active channels and false discovery rate (the null channel selected).
  • Inference. Simulation: paired over replicates (mean difference, 95% t-interval, paired t, exact Wilcoxon, sign counts); equivalence by two one-sided tests (TOST) (Schuirmann, 1987). Real data: 5 outer splits by subject with the Nadeau–Bengio corrected resampled t-interval (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003). In Phase 1 only H1 and H-R12 are confirmatory; everything else is exploratory without multiplicity correction. Phase 2 added four confirmatory hypotheses (Section 5.1).
  • Interpretability (real data). Stability: mean pairwise R² (squared cosine, so sign- and scale-invariant) and mean pairwise cosine of the centred time-averaged effects over 5 splits × 20 bootstrap resamples, on a common 50-point grid between the 5th and 95th percentiles. Clinical alignment: an effect is aligned if it is monotone in the pre-stated direction in at least 80% of these fits.

3. Simulation study

3.1 Design

The simulator (DGP v2) generates n = 1,000 subjects per replicate with three channels on t ∈ [0, 1]: a random level, a random linear trend and four Fourier modes per channel, observed with Gaussian noise at 40 dense times, or at 3–8 random visits in the sparse scenario. Channel 3 is a null channel. Train/validation/test splits are 60/20/20, and censoring is about 30%. Each scenario has 20 replicates on fresh seeds (S-Add 3000–3019, S-Int 3100–3119, S-Sparse 3200–3219, S-Misspec 3300–3319, S-Int/P0 3400–3419).

  • S-Add: η = ∫sin(πX₁(t)/2)dt + ∫tanh(X₂(t))(1 + t)dt + 0.3z₁ − 0.3z₂, Weibull proportional hazards (shape 1.5), so the Cox model is correctly specified. φ₁ is time-homogeneous, φ₂ time-varying.
  • S-Int: S-Add’s η plus 0.8 times the product of the two channels’ functional summaries (a cross-channel interaction that no additive model can represent).
  • S-Sparse: S-Add with 3–8 visits per subject; B-spline smoothing against PACE.
  • S-Misspec: S-Add’s η as an AFT location, log T = 1 − η/1.5 + 0.5ε with log-normal or log-logistic ε (non-PH; the Cox model is misspecified).
  • S-Int/P0: the Round-1 simulator setting P0, re-testing KAN-FAE against MLP-FAE under strategy S3.

The study produced 1243 model records; 3 pruning/symbolic cells failed on a non-finite refit, were rerun after an evaluation-only harness fix, and are reported with their original failed records.

5. Phase 2: pre-registered methodological revisions (Rounds 15–22)

5.1 Motivation and design

Phase 1 left three open problems: the refit after symbolic substitution was unusable (Section 3.6), so the symbolic model could only be used without refit; on the real data the last observed value appeared to carry most of the information (Section 4.2); and the AFCM comparator was not validated (Section 2.4). Four further rounds tested targeted revisions prospectively. Each was registered in the decision log before its seeds were used, after a development dry run on other seeds: Round 15 on S-Add 3500–3519, S-Sparse 3600–3619 and S-Last 3700–3719, Round 16 on S-Add 3800–3819, S-Sparse 3900–3919 and S-Last 4000–4019, and Round 17 on S-Last 4100–4119, S-Sparse 4200–4219 and S-Misspec-lognormal 4300–4319, and Round 18 on S-Last 4400–4419, S-Sparse 4500–4519 and S-Misspec-lognormal 4600–4619, and Round 20 on S-Last 4700–4719, S-Sparse 4800–4819 and S-Misspec-lognormal 4900–4919. The simulator, sample size, splits, front end and ensembles were those of Section 3.

  • Domain guard. A substituted formula containing log, sqrt or 1/x is evaluated with a smooth floor at half the smallest argument seen in training, and exp with a smooth cap 10 above the training range, so a formula cannot leave its domain during a refit.
  • Functional distillation refit. The refit minimises the Cox loss plus μ times the variance-normalised squared distance between the substituted model and the pruned model it summarises (the B2 surfaces at every training subject and grid time, and the static edge activations), so the formulas’ parameters can adapt without drifting from the shapes they approximate. Round 15 chose μ ∈ {1e-2, 1e-1, 1, 10} by validation partial likelihood. Round 16 fixed μ = 10, rejected a library candidate whose inner argument |a x + b| exceeds 50 anywhere on the edge’s training range, and made the refit safe: if it leaves a parameter or the objective non-finite, or does not lower the objective, every parameter is restored to its substituted value (a “fallback”, which is recorded). Each refit used 200 L-BFGS iterations.
  • A landmark-anchored functional model (b2s_anchor_cox). η = Σ_d ψ_d(X_d(t_L)) + Σ_d ∫φ_d(X_d(t), t)dt + Σ_k ψ_k(z_k): a static KAN edge on each channel’s last observed value plus a gated B2-S term on the whole curve. The gate prunes functional channels by their integrated effect over the θ_rel grid of Section 2.5 and also offers “all off” (the model then reduces to a static CoxKAN on the last values). Round 15 chose among these settings with the parsimonious rule of Section 2.5 (tolerance 0.01), under which “all off” wins whenever it is within the tolerance; Round 16’s non-cliff rule applies the same rule only among settings that keep a channel, and takes “all off” only if its validation loss is strictly lower than the best of them. A residual variant (b2s_res_cox; the curve term acts on X(t) − X(t_L)) was a registered secondary in Round 15.
  • Two-way gate (Round 17). The gate also prunes the last-value edges (“joint” states: both branches pruned at the same θ_rel; “static” states: every trajectory channel off) and keeps the plain B2-S model, fitted on the same split, as a candidate. The joint and static states are compared as in Round 16 (non-cliff); the chosen anchored state is used only if its validation loss is below the B2-S model’s by more than a tolerance last_tol, and otherwise the model is the B2-S model itself.
  • Full-validation refit (Round 19, development check only). The Round-18 decision is kept; if anchoring is not confirmed the model is B2-S trained on the whole validation set, and if it is confirmed the chosen masks are refitted on the whole validation set.
  • Confirmation split (Round 18). Each replicate’s validation subjects are split in half, stratified on the event, into val_select and val_confirm. Everything before the final decision uses val_select only, for both the anchored model and the B2-S comparator: tuning, early stopping, the candidate fine-tunes and the choice of the anchored state. The decision itself compares the chosen anchored candidate with the comparator’s first ensemble member on val_confirm, one model against one; anchoring is used only if it wins there by more than a margin, and otherwise the model is the comparator.
  • A semiparametric functional KAN-AFT (NP-KAN-AFT, Round 20). log T = μ + η + ε with η the B2-S location and the law of ε left unspecified: it is the Kaplan–Meier estimate of the training residuals, so the predicted survival is S(t | Z) = S_KM(log t − η(Z)). Two objectives were compared in development: a Buckley–James residual EM (the Kaplan–Meier law re-estimated before every epoch and censored responses replaced by their conditional means) and a smoothed Gehan rank loss (Jose et al., 2025). This is the semiparametric AFT model with a functional KAN location; a Buckley–James KAN-AFT for static covariates is published, so it is not a new model class.
  • Two-half cross-confirmation (Round 20). The Round-18 decision is made in both directions: select on val_select and confirm on val_confirm, and the reverse. In each direction the anchored ensemble (all three seeds) and the B2-S ensemble of the selecting half are compared by their mean member loss on the other half; anchoring is used only if it wins by more than a margin in both directions (relative to the loss for NP-KAN-AFT), and is then fine-tuned once more on the whole validation set; otherwise the model is B2-S trained on the whole validation set.
  • A log-normal KAN-AFT (Round 17). log T = μ + η + σε with ε standard normal, for the comparator (B2-S AFT) and the anchored model, with the same two-way gate and the Round-16 distillation refit (Section 2.2 used a Weibull AFT).
  • A last-value scenario (S-Last). η = sin(πX₁(1)/2) + 1.5 tanh(X₂(1)) + 0.3z₁ − 0.3z₂ with 3–8 visits: the risk depends only on the channels’ values at the end of the window, so a static model on the last values is close to correctly specified.

6. Discussion

6.1 Principal findings

  1. Direct functional KANs are a workable parametrisation of the additive functional Cox model, not a better one. B2-S and AFCM V3 recovered the S-Add shapes to a similar degree and could not be separated by the confirmatory test; both were far better than linear-in-scores two-stage models when the effect is nonlinear.
  2. Interpretability tools work when used conservatively. Channel-level pruning found the active channels (with imperfect removal of the null one); symbolic formulas substituted without refit reproduced the model; leading real-data effects were stable and pointed in clinically expected directions. An unconstrained refit after substitution should be avoided; a refit anchored to the pruned model at a fixed weight, with a fallback and a bound on formula arguments, was safe in every replicate (H-R16a) but did not improve prediction.
  3. No predictive advantage on small real cohorts. Simple landmark models were at least as good; with so few visits per subject, the last observed value plausibly carried most of the information (an interpretation, not a tested finding).
  4. KAN blocks inside an autoencoder did not help. KAN-FAE was worse than or equivalent to MLP-FAE in every comparison, and its edges were not readable as clinical effects.
  5. Anchoring to the last observed value is a trade-off, not a free gain. The anchored model was better than B2-S Cox and landmark Cox when risk depended on the last values, and slightly worse than B2-S Cox when it depended on the trajectory, in Rounds 15 and 16. Neither the non-cliff rule nor a two-way gate that could fall back to B2-S (Round 17) removed the cost reliably on fresh seeds; deciding on a held-out confirmation split with a margin (Round 18) kept most of the gain and stayed within the registered margin, against a comparator that also used only half of the validation data; against B2-S trained on the whole validation set, refitting the anchored model did not keep Cox within the S-Sparse margin (Round 19, development check). Confirming the decision in both halves of the validation split against three-seed B2-S ensembles did keep Cox, the log-normal KAN-AFT and NP-KAN-AFT within the margin against that comparator on fresh seeds (Round 20), mainly by anchoring less often.
  6. The Phase-2 tools carry over to KAN-AFT. For a log-normal KAN-AFT the anchored distillation refit was finite in every replicate and preserved the shapes (H-R17b part 1), as for Cox.
  7. The AFT error law need not be chosen. A semiparametric KAN-AFT, whose error law is the Kaplan–Meier estimate of the residuals, was better than Cox and Weibull models under log-normal errors and as good as the correctly specified log-normal model (H-R20a); it is the classical semiparametric AFT model with a functional KAN location. On the real cohorts it was finite and D-calibrated but did not match the Weibull KAN-AFT on the primary PBC endpoint (H-R21a not supported), and cross-confirmed anchoring stayed within its margin on every cohort (H-R21b). Under error laws that no parametric family in the comparison represents (a bimodal mixture) it was clearly better than all three parametric KAN models, and its advantage shrank as the law approached the normal (H-R22c).
  8. The locked methods transferred to new cohorts without gain. On two cohorts not used in any earlier round they stayed close to the baselines, and anchoring and distillation stayed within their margins; the registered test still failed, because one baseline fit did not converge (H-R22b). A per-channel marginal-likelihood AFCM met the development rule but failed on the held-out seed, so the AFCM comparator remains unvalidated (CK-6d).

6.2 Relation to prior work

Our results are consistent with the view that the additive functional Cox model (Cui et al., 2021) already captures the model form a single-layer functional CoxKAN offers. What KAN parametrisation adds is a uniform toolkit of pruning and symbolic summaries and the option to compose layers; in our interaction scenario the composed B2-deep did not use that option to advantage. KAN survival models with static covariates (Knottenbelt et al., 2025; Jose et al., 2025; Mastroleo et al., 2026; Boulitsakis Logothetis et al., 2026) and the KAN joint model abstract (Lundin et al., 2025) address different input structures, and we make no claim relative to work we could read only in abstract.

6.3 Limitations

  • Unvalidated comparator. AFCM V3 failed its single-fit acceptance check; its shape recovery depends on how the penalties fall on a given data set. AFCM is a Python re-implementation; neither common-λ Laplace marginal-likelihood selection (Round 15) nor per-channel REML-type selection by the Fellner–Schall update (Round 22, which passed the three development seeds but failed CK-6d on the held-out seed) repaired it, and mgcv itself could not be run (Section 5.10). H-R12 therefore stays inconclusive.
  • Phase 2 was motivated post hoc. The Round-15 to Round-17 revisions were designed after looking at earlier rounds’ failures (on other seeds), tested on one simulator, and the Round-16 and Round-17 changes were not run on real data; the Round-15 real-data analyses used the same data that motivated the anchored design. Round 21 ran the locked Round-20 methods and the fixed-μ distillation on the real data, on the same splits as Rounds 13 and 15, so those results are an application to known data rather than a fresh test.
  • Tolerances tuned on few seeds. Round 17’s last_tol values were chosen on the already-used Round-16 replicates (and development seeds for S-Misspec); the Cox value did not transfer to fresh seeds (H-R17a), and the AFT value lies at the edge of its grid. Round 18’s margins were also tuned in-sample and also lie at the edge of the grid.
  • The confirmation split halves the validation data. Round 18’s comparator was trained on val_select; against the usual full-validation B2-S Cox, the S-Sparse non-inferiority of the confirmation-gated model was not shown, and the Round-19 refit did not change that in development (H-R19a and H-R19b were not tested). Round 20’s cross-confirmation met the margin against the full-validation comparator on fresh seeds.
  • Round-20 settings chosen on reused seeds. The NP-KAN-AFT objective and the margins were chosen on development seeds used for the third time, and the Cox margin lies at the edge of its grid. The Round-19 miss and the Round-20 pass are on different seeds, so the cross-confirmation is supported by a fresh-seed test but not shown to be the cause of the improvement; the conservative Cox margin also gave up part of the S-Last gain.
  • NP-KAN-AFT was tested under one error law. Its advantage over the Cox and Weibull models reflects their misspecification under log-normal errors; it was not better than the correct parametric family, and its predicted survival does not extrapolate beyond the largest training residual.
  • Round-21 real-data results reuse known splits. The four cohorts and their five outer splits were used in Rounds 13 and 15, so Round 21 applied fixed methods to known data; five splits cannot power superiority tests, and the C-MAPSS thresholds of H-R21c were stated before the run without a matching earlier result.
  • One simulator, 20 replicates. H-R12 rests on one DGP with three channels and n = 1,000.
  • Small real-data cohorts with few visits, so all intervals are wide; five outer splits give limited resolution.
  • PACE failed on one PBC split (non-finite curves on a gappy visit design); the PACE sensitivity row has 4 PBC E2 splits.
  • Coarse clinical alignment. Monotonicity over the 5th–95th percentiles is a crude check; clinical sign-off is external, as is the choice of the PBC endpoint.
  • No joint model or deep dynamic model (JM, Dynamic-DeepHit) was fitted; the two-stage mixed-effects Cox model stands in for the joint-model class.
  • Framing (b) is conditional on configurations selected and gated for the MLP; it shows that the KAN encoder did not help under these configurations, not that it cannot.
  • Round-22 external data. The renal and heart-valve cohorts were untouched, but heart valve had a median of one visit per patient before the landmark and one marker missing for some patients, so it says little about trajectory models; FD003 had been analysed in Round 3 and is not untouched; five splits per cohort cannot power superiority claims.
  • Error laws are simulated. The three Round-22 error laws share one location model and one censoring mechanism; they test robustness of the error-law estimate, not of the functional effect.
  • Targeted literature review. No systematic review was done, so no novelty claim is made.

6.4 Reproducibility

All code, decision logs, pre-registrations, raw records and this manuscript are in one repository. Public data were obtained from CRAN (survival 3.8-12, JM 1.5-2, joineRML 0.4.8) and NASA’s prognostics repository, with checksums in the run manifest. Each round’s plan was committed before its seeds were used. Every number in this manuscript is rendered from the saved records, logged with its source, and re-checked by an independent script and a unit test (Appendix A).

7. Conclusions

Functional CoxKAN and KAN-AFT models that apply KAN edges directly to longitudinal trajectories recover nonlinear functional effects, support channel selection and give accurate symbolic summaries when the formulas are substituted without refitting. In our pre-registered comparisons they were not better than the additive functional Cox model (whose re-implementation could not itself be validated) or than simple landmark baselines on small clinical cohorts, and burying KAN blocks inside a functional autoencoder gave neither better prediction nor clinically readable effects. A second, pre-registered phase made the symbolic refit safe (a strongly anchored, NaN-safe distillation refit), at the cost of making it nearly inert, and showed that anchoring a functional model to the last observed values helps when the last value carries the risk and costs a little when the trajectory does; a two-way gate did not remove that cost reliably for Cox, and a cautious gate for a log-normal KAN-AFT avoided it only by giving up most of the gain; deciding on a held-out confirmation split met every registered criterion for both, at the price of halving the validation data available to each model, and against B2-S trained on the whole validation set it could not be shown to avoid the S-Sparse cost for Cox. Confirming the decision in both halves of the validation split against three-seed B2-S ensembles closed that gap on fresh seeds, for Cox and AFT models, by anchoring less often, and a semiparametric KAN-AFT with a Kaplan–Meier error law performed as well as the correctly specified parametric model without having to know it, and was clearly better when no parametric family fitted the error law. On the real cohorts, including two not used before, these methods were safe but not better than the simpler models. These negative, equivalence and trade-off findings, with the full record behind them, are the contribution.
